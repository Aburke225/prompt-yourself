Guide 01 · Learn

Learn any topic with AI

You do not need a paid course to learn something new. A free AI chat can be a patient tutor for almost any topic — budgeting, anatomy, algebra, wine, or plumbing. The only skill you need is knowing what to say to it. This page teaches that skill.

Part 1

Ask to be taught, not just answered

There are two ways to use an AI chat. You can ask it for answers. Or you can ask it to teach you.

Asking for answers feels faster. You get a clean summary in seconds. But a week later, most of it is gone. You read the answer — you never used it. Reading alone does not build memory.

Learning happens when your brain does work. You try to recall something. You explain it in your own words. You make a mistake and fix it. That effort is what makes knowledge stay.

So switch the roles. Do not treat the AI like a search box. Treat it like a patient tutor with unlimited time for you. Every technique on this page does one thing: it moves the work from the AI to you.

Part 2

Start every chat with three facts about you

A good human tutor asks about you before the first lesson. An AI will not ask unless you tell it to. So begin every learning chat with three facts:

  • Your level. "I am a complete beginner." "I know the basics but get confused after that."
  • Your goal. "I want to pass my licensing exam in June." "I want to understand my own tax form."
  • How you like to learn. Short explanations? Lots of examples? Practice questions?

Then add the most important rule: ask it to check your understanding instead of lecturing. Here is a starting prompt you can reuse for any topic:

You are my tutor for [TOPIC].
My level: [COMPLETE BEGINNER / I KNOW THE BASICS / DESCRIBE IT].
My goal: [WHAT YOU WANT TO BE ABLE TO DO].
I learn best with [SHORT EXPLANATIONS / EXAMPLES / PRACTICE QUESTIONS].

Important: do not lecture. Explain one small piece at a time.
After each piece, ask me one question to check I understood.
Wait for my answer before you continue.

That last rule is the most important one. The chat stops being one long block of text. It becomes a conversation where you have to think.

One more habit: save your filled-in prompt in a notes file on your phone or computer. At the start of each session, paste it instead of writing it again. And when your level changes, change the prompt — "complete beginner" should not still be there in month three.

Part 3

Six techniques that work for any topic

These six moves work for nursing, retail management, law, music theory, coding — anything. Copy a template, fill in the brackets, and send it. You do not need all six every time. A good session often looks like: plan, explain, teach back, quiz, practice.

1. Build a learning plan

Before you study, ask for a plan. Ask for steps small enough to finish in one study session, with a clear way to know each step is done.

I want to learn [TOPIC].
My goal: [WHAT YOU WANT TO BE ABLE TO DO] by [WHEN].
Time I can spend: [HOURS PER WEEK].
What I already know: [NOTHING / A LITTLE / DESCRIBE IT].

Make me a step-by-step learning plan.
Keep each step small enough for one study session.
For each step, tell me how I will know I am done with it.
Then ask me if any step looks too big.

2. Explain at my level, then raise the level

Start simple, and only go deeper when the simple version is clear. This stops the most common failure: an explanation that is correct but too advanced to help you.

Explain [CONCEPT] to me.
Round 1: explain it for a complete beginner. Everyday words only.
Then ask me what is still unclear, and wait for my answer.
Round 2: explain it again, one level deeper.
Keep going one level at a time until I say stop.

3. Quiz me, one question at a time

Trying to recall an answer is one of the best-tested ways to learn. The key is one question at a time — otherwise you read the answers instead of trying to recall them.

Quiz me on [TOPIC].
Ask ONE question at a time and wait for my answer.
If I am right, tell me why the answer is right.
If I am wrong, do not give me the answer yet.
Give me a hint and let me try once more.
Start easy and get harder.
After 10 questions, list what I should review.

4. Make me teach it back

Explaining something in your own words is the strongest test of understanding. If you cannot explain it, you have not learned it yet — and the AI will show you exactly where the gap is.

I will now explain [CONCEPT] in my own words.
Read it like a strict but kind teacher.
Tell me: what I got right, what I got wrong,
and what important part I left out.

My explanation: [WRITE YOUR EXPLANATION HERE]

5. Ask for analogies and worked examples

A new idea is easier to remember when it connects to something you already know. Tell the AI what you know well, and make it walk through a full example — not just the steps, but the reasons.

Explain [CONCEPT] using an analogy from [SOMETHING YOU KNOW WELL, for example: cooking, football, gardening, running a small shop].
Then walk me through one worked example, step by step.
Say why each step happens, not just what to do.
Then give me one similar example to try by myself.

6. Practice, and let it correct you

Mistakes are not a problem. Mistakes with instant, clear feedback are how skill grows. This is the loop to repeat the most.

Give me one practice task on [TOPIC] at [MY LEVEL].
Wait for my answer.
Then correct it like a good teacher:
point to each mistake, explain why it is wrong,
and show me the better version.
Then give me a similar task so I can try again.

Extra practice: find the hidden mistakes

When a topic starts to feel familiar, reverse the roles: the AI makes the mistakes, and you do the correcting. Finding errors is a different skill from giving answers, and it is a fast test of how solid your understanding really is.

Write a short explanation of [CONCEPT] and hide 3 mistakes in it.
Do not tell me where they are.
I will try to find all 3.
After my answer, reveal each mistake and explain why it is wrong.

Part 4

A 30-minute study session

You do not need hours. Thirty minutes with a clear plan is better than two hours without one. Here is one plan, built from the six techniques above. The minutes are a guide, not a rule.

  1. Minutes 1–3: set up. Paste your starting prompt from Part 2. Add one line about what you studied last time.
  2. Minutes 3–8: recall first. Before anything new, ask for a short quiz on your last session (technique 3). Starting with recall brings old knowledge back before you build on it.
  3. Minutes 8–18: learn one new piece. Ask for one explanation at your level (technique 2). Ask questions until it is clear. One piece, not three.
  4. Minutes 18–24: teach it back. Explain the new piece in your own words and let the AI correct you (technique 4).
  5. Minutes 24–29: practice. Ask for one practice task and get it corrected (technique 6).
  6. Minute 30: close. Ask: "List what I got wrong today, in one line each." Save that list — you will need it later on this page.

Notice what is missing: no long reading and no copying of notes. Almost every minute makes you recall, explain, or try. That is what makes it work.

Part 5

Check what the AI tells you

AI chats sometimes say things that are wrong — and they say them with full confidence. This is a known limit of the technology, not a reason to avoid it. It just means you need a small checking habit — the same way you would not repeat a fact from a stranger without checking it first.

  • Ask the AI itself: "How sure are you about this? What should I double-check?" It often knows which parts might be wrong.
  • Ask for sources — and open them. If a source does not exist, or does not say what the AI claimed, treat that claim as unconfirmed.
  • Check important facts twice. Names, numbers, dates, prices, laws, and health advice deserve a quick search on the web: an official site, Wikipedia, or your library.
  • Match the effort to the risk. Learning for fun? A quick check is fine. Making a decision about money, health, law, or an exam? Confirm with a trusted source before you act.
  • Watch the date. An AI's knowledge stops at a certain date. For things that change often — prices, laws, software menus — check a current source.

Ideas, explanations, and practice questions from an AI are usually good. Specific facts are what you check. Remember that difference, and you get a tutor you can trust.

One more check for facts that matter: open a new chat and ask the same question in different words. If the two answers do not agree, at least one of them is wrong — confirm with a trusted source before you use either.

Part 6

Remember it next week

One good session is not enough. Most of what you learn today fades within a week — unless you come back to it. The fix is simple, and it takes minutes, not hours.

  • Tomorrow: a five-minute quiz on what you studied. No re-reading first. Recall, then check.
  • Next week: quiz again, and add questions from older topics too. Old material also needs review.
  • Keep a mistake list. After each session, write down what you got wrong — one line each. This short list is worth more than pages of notes, because it shows exactly what to review.

A new chat starts empty. It does not know what you studied before or what you got wrong. Your mistake list carries that memory for you. Paste it in and let the AI build the review from it:

I am learning [TOPIC]. Here are things I got wrong before:
[PASTE YOUR MISTAKE LIST]

Quiz me on these, one question at a time.
Use new wording, so I cannot answer from memory of the old question.
When I get an item right two times, tell me I can remove it from my list.
At the end, tell me what to add to the list from today.

Watch the list over the weeks. A list that keeps getting shorter is progress you can see.

Part 7

Free tools to pair with your AI chat

Everything on this page works with free tools. All of these were checked in September 2026.

Free AI chats

  • Duck.ai — free, no account needed. One site where you can chat with several well-known AI models. Chats are saved on your device, not to an account. There is a daily limit on free use, but it is enough for a study session. The easiest place to start.
  • ChatGPT — basic text chat works without an account. Chats are not saved in that mode, so copy anything you want to keep. A free account (no payment needed) adds saved chats and more features.
  • Claude — free with an account. Good at long, patient explanations. The free plan limits how many messages you can send in a few hours.
  • Gemini — free with a Google account. The free plan has daily limits.

Any of these can run every prompt on this page. If you do not want to sign up for anything, use Duck.ai.

Free learning sites

  • YouTube — free, no account needed to watch most videos. When the AI's text explanation is not enough, ask it: "What should I search on YouTube to see this done?"
  • Wikipedia — free, no account. Good for checking facts and for learning the standard names for things in your topic.
  • Khan Academy — free. No account needed to watch; a free account saves your progress. Strong for math, science, economics, and test preparation.
  • MIT OpenCourseWare — free, no account. Real university course materials: lecture notes, problem sets, and videos, from beginner to advanced.
  • Your public library — free with a library card. Many libraries also give free online access to e-books, courses, and language tools. Ask a librarian what you can get with your card.

A pattern that works well: the AI chat is your tutor, and these sites are your textbook and your fact-checker.

Part 8

A full example: reading a rental contract

Here is the whole method on one everyday topic: understanding a rental contract before you sign it. Nothing technical — just a thing many people sign without fully reading.

You: You are my tutor. Topic: rental contracts. My level: I have never signed one. My goal: understand the contract for an apartment I want to rent, so I know what I am agreeing to. Do not lecture — explain one part at a time and check my understanding as we go.

The AI: Explains one small idea: a rental contract mainly answers four questions — how much you pay, how long you stay, what you must do, and what the landlord must do. Then it asks you: "Which of those four do you think causes the most arguments?"

You: You guess "the rent." It says you were close — deposits cause more arguments — and explains why with a short example. Then you switch to teach-back: "Let me explain a security deposit in my own words. Tell me what I get wrong."

The AI: Points out the part you missed — the exact rules for when a landlord can keep part of the deposit — and shows a simple example with numbers.

You: "Quiz me. Five short questions about deposits, notice periods, and repairs. One at a time."

After the quiz, you ask one last question: "What should I ask the landlord before I sign?" Then comes the checking habit. Deposit and notice rules differ by country and city, so you confirm the local rules on an official housing or tenant-information site before you rely on them.

Notice the loop: tell the AI about you, get one small explanation, teach it back, take a quiz, then check the facts that matter. The same loop works for personal budgeting, a nursing exam, a new language, or a new job skill.

Part 9

Five mistakes to avoid

The method on this page is simple, but a few habits can quietly undo it. Watch for these five.

  • Asking for a summary and moving on. A summary feels like learning, but reading is not using. Turn every summary into a quiz or a teach-back before you leave it.
  • Accepting the first explanation. If it is not fully clear, say so, and ask: "Explain it again, with a different example." A human tutor may get tired of repeating things. The AI never does.
  • Letting the AI do your homework. If a class or a job gave you the task, you learn by doing it yourself. Ask for hints, steps, and corrections — not the finished answer. You can always say: "Give me a hint, not the solution."
  • Studying five things at once. One clear piece per session is better than five unclear ones. If a step in your learning plan feels too big, ask the AI to split it.
  • Never coming back. A topic visited once is a topic your memory is already losing. The short return visits described earlier are where learning becomes permanent.

If you notice one of these habits, you do not need to start over. Ask one recall question about yesterday's topic. You are learning again.
